## One agent, three jobs, 22% of tickets wrong

Your support agent handles everything: refunds, technical problems and sales questions. It has one system prompt with the refund policy, the troubleshooting guide and the pricing rules, about 6,000 tokens, and all four tools: `lookup_order`, `issue_refund`, `search_docs` and `get_pricing`.

On a test set of 100 tickets it gets 78 right. Only about 55% of its tool calls hit the right tool. A customer writes "Since the update the app crashes on login. This is costing me money." The agent looks up their last order and refunds a month as goodwill. The crash is still there.

## Too many options, one blurry prompt

The model is not careless. It is choosing among four tools using a prompt that tries to cover every case at once. "This is costing me money" sounds like a refund ticket. A refund request that says "what is your policy?" sounds like a help-center question, so it calls `search_docs` and quotes the policy instead of refunding.

Every extra tool is another wrong option, and every extra page of instructions dilutes the ones that matter for *this* ticket. The prompt is also expensive: all 6,000 tokens ride along on every turn, even though any one ticket needs only a third of them.

## Classify first, then hand it to a specialist

Routing splits the job in two. First, a **router** decides what kind of request this is. Then a **specialist** for that kind handles it, with a short prompt and only its own tools.

<Flow steps={["Ticket", { label: "Router", sub: "small model: refund / tech / sales", kind: "hl" }, { label: "Specialist", sub: "short prompt, 1-2 tools" }, "Answer"]} caption="The router outputs one label. Each route has its own prompt, tools and even model tier." />

```python
ROUTES = {
    "refund": (REFUND_PROMPT, [lookup_order, issue_refund]),
    "tech":   (TECH_PROMPT,   [search_docs]),
    "sales":  (SALES_PROMPT,  [get_pricing]),
}
label = small_model(f"Classify as refund, tech, sales or other:\n{ticket}").strip()
if label not in ROUTES:
    return escalate_to_human(ticket)
prompt, tools = ROUTES[label]
return run_loop(model, tools, ticket, system=prompt)
```

The router can be tiny. Picking one of three labels is an easy task, so a small model (or even keyword rules plus a model for the hard cases) does it well. Each specialist now sees about 2,000 tokens of focused instructions and cannot reach for a tool that does not belong to its route.

Routing also works on difficulty, not just topic. Anthropic's post suggests sending easy, common questions to a small model and hard ones to a large model.

## Let us check the math

Sim prices (illustrative): small $1/$5, medium $3/$15 per million input/output tokens. A ticket takes about 3 turns.

<Callout type="math" label="Let us check the math">
**Generalist:** 6,000-token prompt × 3 turns = 18,000 input tokens of prompt alone.<br />
18,000 × $3/M = **$0.054 per ticket** in instructions.<br /><br />
**Routed:** router call on the small tier, 300 in and 10 out: $0.0003 + $0.00005 ≈ **$0.0004**.<br />
Specialist: 2,000 × 3 turns = 6,000 tokens × $3/M = **$0.018**.<br />
Total ≈ **$0.018 per ticket**, a third of the cost.<br /><br />
**Accuracy:** the router is right 95% of the time, the specialist 97%.<br />
End to end: 0.95 × 0.97 ≈ **0.92**, against 0.78 for the generalist.
</Callout>

Notice the multiplication. The router's accuracy caps the whole system. A specialist cannot fix a ticket it never receives.

## The tradeoff: a misroute fails quietly

Routing adds one more thing that can be wrong, and its errors are hard to see:

- **Misroutes.** A refund sent to tech gets a polite troubleshooting answer and no refund. Add an `other` route that goes to a generalist or a human ([Human in the Loop](/c/hitl/)), and ask the router for its confidence.
- **Mixed tickets.** "Refund me, and also how do I export to CSV?" belongs to two routes. Either route on the first intent and let the specialist hand off ([Handoffs and Shared State](/c/handoffs/)), or split the ticket.
- **Drift.** Routes that worked in March break when you launch a new product. Keep a labelled set of tickets and measure router accuracy on its own ([Why Evals Come First](/c/evals/)).

## In the real world

Help desks routed tickets long before LLMs: a rule or a person tagged each ticket and sent it to the billing, tech or sales queue. AI support agents keep that step. A typical design, like the one in [Design a Customer Support Agent](/c/case_support_agent/), classifies intent first and only then picks the procedure, tools and model for that intent.
