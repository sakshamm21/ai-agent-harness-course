## Your judge says 85% of replies are fine. Humans say 60%.

Your support agent writes 3,000 free-text replies a day. String checks from [Why Evals Come First](/c/evals/) cannot grade them: "I'm afraid that's outside the window" and "sorry, too late for a refund" mean the same thing and share no words. So you add a small model as a judge. It reads each draft and a rubric and says pass or fail.

The dashboard reports 85% passing. Then your team hand-labels 100 of the same drafts, and only 60 are actually good. The judge waved 25 bad drafts through, including a reply with a tracking number that does not exist.

## A judge is another model, so it makes mistakes too

A judge does not see the truth. It sees a draft and a prompt, and it predicts what a grader would say. That prediction has its own errors:

- **Too lenient.** A low bar passes anything that sounds polite and confident. Confident wrong answers are exactly what you need it to catch.
- **Too strict.** A bar near perfect rejects good drafts too, and each rejection costs another model call.
- **Biased.** Judges tend to prefer longer answers, the first answer when shown two, and answers in their own style.

Until you compare the judge with people, you do not know which of these you have. A judge you have not checked is an opinion, not a measurement.

## Calibrate the judge against human labels

Label a sample by hand once. Then run the judge on the same sample and count where they agree and disagree.

<Flow steps={[{ label: "100 drafts", sub: "labelled by people" }, { label: "Judge", sub: "same 100 drafts", kind: "hl" }, { label: "Compare", sub: "agreement, kappa, misses" }, "Adjust rubric or bar"]} loop loopLabel="re-measure after every judge change" caption="Treat the judge like any other model: evaluate it before you rely on it." />

Read three numbers, not one:

- **Agreement rate:** how often judge and humans give the same grade.
- **Catch rate on bad drafts:** of the drafts humans failed, how many did the judge fail? This is the number that protects customers.
- **Cohen's kappa:** agreement after subtracting what chance alone would give. A judge that passes everything can still look 60% "accurate".

Then tune: tighten the rubric ("fail any reply that promises a refund the policy does not allow"), adjust the pass bar, and measure again. In the lab, the bar is a slider. Too low misses bad drafts; too high bounces good ones and burns calls.

## Let us check the math

Humans labelled 100 drafts: 60 pass, 40 fail. Here is the judge before and after calibration.

<Callout type="math" label="Let us check the math">
**Before (lenient bar):** judge passes 83. Of the 60 good drafts it passes 58; of the 40 bad drafts it passes 25 and fails 15.<br />
Agreement = (58 + 15) / 100 = **73%**. Catch rate on bad drafts = 15 / 40 = **37.5%**.<br />
Chance agreement = 0.83 × 0.60 + 0.17 × 0.40 = 0.498 + 0.068 = 0.566.<br />
Kappa = (0.73 − 0.566) / (1 − 0.566) = 0.164 / 0.434 ≈ **0.38**.<br /><br />
**After (tighter rubric and bar):** judge passes 60. Good drafts: 56 pass, 4 fail. Bad drafts: 4 pass, 36 fail.<br />
Agreement = (56 + 36) / 100 = **92%**. Catch rate = 36 / 40 = **90%**.<br />
Chance agreement = 0.6 × 0.6 + 0.4 × 0.4 = 0.52. Kappa = (0.92 − 0.52) / 0.48 ≈ **0.83**.<br /><br />
Cost: a small-tier judge reads about 1,900 tokens and writes 200 per draft: 1,900 × $1/M + 200 × $5/M ≈ **$0.003 per draft**, or about $9 a day for 3,000 drafts at sim prices (illustrative).
</Callout>

The lenient judge looked fine on raw agreement and caught barely a third of the bad drafts. Kappa and the catch rate show it.

## The tradeoff: you now have two models to maintain

A judge scales grading from 100 drafts to 100,000, but it adds cost and its own failure modes:

- **It drifts.** Change the judge's prompt or model and its agreement changes. Keep the labelled set and re-run it, like any [eval](/c/evals/).
- **It has biases.** When comparing two answers, grade both orders and keep only consistent verdicts. Give it a rubric with concrete fail conditions, not "is this good?".
- **It adds a call in the loop.** Used as a gate that sends drafts back, a strict judge multiplies calls per task. That pattern is [Evaluator-Optimizer](/c/evaluator_optimizer/); cap its retries.

```python
def calibrate(judge, labelled):              # [(draft, human_pass), ...]
    pairs = [(judge(d), h) for d, h in labelled]
    n = len(pairs)
    agree = sum(j == h for j, h in pairs) / n
    bad = [j for j, h in pairs if not h]
    catch = sum(not j for j in bad) / len(bad)
    pj = sum(j for j, _ in pairs) / n         # judge pass rate
    ph = sum(h for _, h in pairs) / n         # human pass rate
    chance = pj * ph + (1 - pj) * (1 - ph)
    kappa = (agree - chance) / (1 - chance)
    return agree, catch, kappa                # re-run on every judge change
```

## In the real world

Zheng et al. (2023) built MT-Bench, a set of 80 multi-turn questions, and compared GPT-4 as a judge with expert human votes. GPT-4 agreed with the humans over 80% of the time, about as often as the humans agreed with each other. The same paper measured position bias, verbosity bias and self-enhancement bias, and showed that swapping answer order and giving the judge reference answers reduce them. That is the practice to copy: use the judge, and keep checking it against people.
